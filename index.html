This unit covers the everyday expressions you use to greet people, say goodbye, thank someone, apologize, and get someone's attention. Below is what each expression is for and when to use it.

🌅 Greeting by time of day

Ohayou gozaimasu, Konnichiwa, and Konbanwa are not interchangeable — each one belongs to a specific part of the day. Use Ohayou gozaimasu in the morning (roughly until 10–11 am), Konnichiwa from late morning through the afternoon, and Konbanwa in the evening, once it gets dark. Oyasumi nasai is used only at night, right before someone goes to sleep — not as a general "goodbye."

👋 Ending a conversation

Sayounara is used when parting ways, often for a longer time or in a more formal context (for example, after a lesson with a teacher). Ja mata is casual, used with friends when you expect to see them again soon. Mata ashita is used specifically when you know you will see the person again tomorrow (for example, at the end of a work or school day).

🙏 Thanking someone

Arigatou gozaimasu is the polite, standard way to thank someone — safe to use with strangers, teachers, or in shops. Arigatou drops the polite ending and is used only with friends, family, or people of equal/lower status; using it with a stranger or superior can sound too casual.

🙇 Apologizing and getting attention

Sumimasen is one of the most useful words in Japanese because it covers several situations: calling a waiter or shop assistant, stepping past someone, or apologizing for a minor inconvenience. Gomen nasai is a more direct, personal apology, used with people you know when you've actually done something wrong — it is too casual/intimate for strangers or formal settings.

🤲 Making a polite request

Onegaishimasu is attached after naming what you want (e.g., an item, a favor) to make the request polite. It is used in shops, restaurants, and any situation where you are asking for something from someone you don't know well.

😊 Reassuring someone

Daijoubu desu is used to say that a situation is fine — for example, responding to someone's apology to tell them not to worry, or saying that you yourself are okay after being asked.

Japanese greetings often change according to the time of day and the level of politeness. The expression gozaimasu makes an expression more polite — you will hear it added to many basic phrases in formal or respectful situations (for example, with teachers, shopkeepers, or people you are meeting for the first time). Among close friends or family, the shorter, casual forms (such as Arigatou instead of Arigatou gozaimasu) are much more common.

This unit covers what to say when you don't understand something, when you need someone to repeat or slow down, and how to ask about words or languages.

🤔 Saying you understand / don't understand

Wakarimasu ("I understand") and Wakarimasen ("I do not understand") are used on their own as full responses — for example, after someone explains something to you. Adding Yoku ("well") in front, as in Yoku wakarimasen, softens it to "I don't really understand," which sounds more polite than a flat "I don't understand."

🔁 Asking someone to repeat

Mou ichido onegaishimasu ("one more time, please") is the simple, polite way to ask for a repeat — useful anywhere, including shops and classrooms. Mou ichido itte kudasai is slightly more specific ("please say it one more time") and is common in a learning context, such as with a teacher or tutor.

🐢 Asking someone to slow down

Yukkuri onegaishimasu is a short, polite request to slow down. Yukkuri hanashite kudasai spells it out more directly ("please speak slowly") and is useful when the first, shorter request didn't work.

🌍 Asking if someone speaks a language

The pattern [language] ga dekimasu ka? is used to ask whether someone can speak a given language — swap in any language name (see the Languages table below, e.g. Nihongo, Doitsugo, Furansugo) depending on who you're asking. Answer with Hai, dekimasu ("yes, I can") or Iie, dekimasen ("no, I cannot"). Chotto dekimasu ("I can speak a little") is a common, modest middle answer.

👉 Pointing things out: kore / sore / are

Use kore for something close to you, sore for something close to the person you're talking to, and are for something far from both of you — useful whenever you're pointing at an object and don't know its name yet. When the word comes right before a noun ("this book", "that cup"), switch to kono/sono/ano + noun instead.

❔ Asking with question words

Nan/nani ("what"), dare ("who"), doko ("where"), itsu ("when"), dou ("how"), dore ("which one"), and naze/doushite ("why") are the core question words you'll need from day one — for example, pointing at something and asking Kore wa nan desu ka? ("What is this?"), or asking Doko desu ka? ("Where is it?") when looking for a place.

❓ Asking how to say or read something

Kore wa Nihongo de nan to iimasu ka? ("how do you say this in Japanese?") is used when you want a translation into Japanese; swap Nihongo for Eigo to ask for English instead. Kore wa nan desu ka? simply asks "what is this?" when pointing at an object. Dou yomu n desu ka? and Nani to yomimasu ka? both ask specifically how a written word is read/pronounced, which is useful when you can see a word but don't know how to say it.

1. Saying that you understand or do not understand

Wakarimasu. — I understand.

Wakarimasen. — I do not understand.

Yoku wakarimasen. — I do not really understand.

The word yoku means "well" in this expression.

How this word is built: the dictionary (base) form is wakaru ("to understand/become clear"). To make a polite sentence, Japanese verbs attach a suffix to a stem instead of using a separate "to be" word:
• wakar- + -imasu → wakarimasu = "I understand" (polite, present/future, affirmative)
• wakar- + -imasen → wakarimasen = "I do not understand" (polite, present/future, negative)
So -masu is the polite affirmative ending and -masen is its negative counterpart — this same pattern (verb stem + masu/masen) is used for almost every polite Japanese verb, not just wakaru. You cannot drop -masu/-masen and keep the sentence polite: saying just "wakari" is not a complete, natural sentence on its own. In casual speech among close friends, people instead use the plain forms wakaru (understand) and wakaranai (don't understand) — but as an A1 beginner, stick to the polite -masu/-masen forms, which are safe in every situation.

2. Asking someone to repeat something

Mou ichido onegaishimasu. — One more time, please.

Mou ichido itte kudasai. — Please say it one more time.

3. Asking someone to speak slowly

Yukkuri onegaishimasu. — Slowly, please.

Yukkuri hanashite kudasai. — Please speak slowly.

4. Asking if someone speaks a language

Nihongo ga dekimasu ka? — Can you speak Japanese?

Eigo ga dekimasu ka? — Can you speak English?

Hai, dekimasu. — Yes, I can.

Iie, dekimasen. — No, I cannot.

The pattern "language + ga dekimasu ka" is used to ask whether someone can speak a language. The same -masu/-masen pattern from point 1 is at work here too: dekimasu ("can do" — affirmative) vs. dekimasen ("cannot do" — negative).

5. "This / that / these" — kore, sore, are, kono, sono, ano

Japanese has three "distance" words instead of English's two ("this" vs. "that"):

  • Kore — this (the thing is close to the speaker)
  • Sore — that (the thing is close to the listener, not the speaker)
  • Are — that over there (far from both speaker and listener)

Kore/sore/are stand alone as "this/that [thing]" (e.g. Kore wa nan desu ka? — "What is this?"). When you want to say "this ___" directly in front of a noun, use the matching kono/sono/ano + noun form instead — you cannot use kore/sore/are directly before a noun.

Kore wa pen desu. — This is a pen.
Kono pen wa watashi no desu. — This pen is mine.

The same pattern extends to place: koko (here), soko (there), asoko (over there).

6. Question words — nan/nani, dare, doko, itsu, dou, dore...

Nan and nani both mean "what" — nan is used right before desu ka or a counter (e.g. nan desu ka?), while nani is used in most other positions (e.g. nani o shimasu ka? — "what will you do?").

  • Dare — who (e.g. Dare desu ka? — "Who is it?")
  • Doko — where (e.g. Doko desu ka? — "Where is it?")
  • Itsu — when
  • Dou — how / in what way (e.g. Dou yomu n desu ka? — "How do you read this?")
  • Dore — which one, when choosing among three or more
  • Ikura — how much (asking a price)
  • Naze / Doushite — why

A question word normally replaces the piece of information you're asking about, and the sentence still ends in ka to mark it as a question — this is the same ka you see in dekimasu ka? and nan desu ka?.

7. Asking how to say something in Japanese

Kore wa Nihongo de nan to iimasu ka? — How do you say this in Japanese?

Eigo de nan to iimasu ka? — How do you say it in English?

Kore wa nan desu ka? — What is this?

8. Asking about pronunciation

Dou yomu n desu ka? — How do you read this?

Nani to yomimasu ka? — How do you read this?

This unit covers how to say your name, nationality, job, and where you're from, and how to ask someone else the same things — everything you need for a short self-introduction.

👤 Introducing your name

Watashi wa [name] desu ("I am [name]") is the basic pattern for introducing yourself. In natural, relaxed speech, watashi wa is often dropped once it's clear you're talking about yourself — just [Name] desu is common and perfectly polite.

🌍 Saying your nationality and where you're from

[Nationality] desu states your nationality directly (e.g. Torukojin desu — "I am Turkish"). [Country] kara kimashita ("I came from [country]") describes your origin as a journey rather than an identity — both are common in a self-introduction. [Place] shusshin desu ("I am from [place]") is used for a more specific hometown or city, not just a country.

💼 Talking about your job

Occupation is stated the same way as nationality or name: [Job] desu (e.g. Gakusei desu — "I am a student"). There's no separate verb for "to work as" at this level — the same desu pattern covers name, nationality, and job.

❓ Asking someone's name and hometown

Onamae wa nan desu ka? ("What is your name?") is the full, polite question; the o- prefix on namae makes it more polite when asking about someone else (you don't add it when talking about your own name). A shorter O-namae wa? ("And your name?") works well once the context is clear, such as right after introducing yourself. Shusshin wa doko desu ka? and Doko kara kimashita ka? both ask where someone is from.

🙅 Saying what you are not

[Noun] ja nai desu is the polite negative of desu — "am/is/are not." It works the same way for name, nationality, or job: Gakusei ja nai desu ("I am not a student"), Nihonjin ja nai desu ("I am not Japanese").

🎤 Putting it together

A full self-introduction strings several of these patterns together in a fixed order: greeting → name → nationality → origin → closing greeting. Hajimemashite ("nice to meet you," said only the first time you meet someone) opens it, and Douzo yoroshiku onegaishimasu (also "nice to meet you," but more like "please treat me well") closes it.

6. The particle "wa"

The particle wa marks the topic of the sentence.

Watashi wa Ayse desu. — As for me, I am Ayse.

It is pronounced "wa," although its spelling in Japanese writing is different.

7. The copula "desu"

Desu is used to say "am," "is," or "are" in polite sentences.

Gakusei desu. — I am a student.

Toruko jin desu. — I am Turkish.

The pattern ja nai desu means "am not," "is not," or "are not."

Gakusei ja nai desu. — I am not a student.

Nihonjin ja nai desu. — I am not Japanese.

9. The particle "kara"

Kara means "from" when talking about a place.

Toruko kara kimashita. — I came from Turkey.

Ankara kara kimashita. — I came from Ankara.

This unit covers talking about your family, saying and asking ages, saying where you live, and describing the people around you.

👨‍👩‍👧 Talking about your family

Kazoku wa [number] nin desu states how many people are in your family. Japanese uses different words depending on whose relative it is: Chichi/Haha ("my father/mother") versus Otousan/Okaasan ("someone else's father/mother," also used to address your own parents directly or more politely). The particle to ("and") links family members in a list: Chichi to haha — "my father and mother."

🔗 Saying someone's relationship to you

The particle no shows possession or connection, much like "'s" or "of" in English: Watashi no haha ("my mother"), Yuki-san no tomodachi ("Yuki's friend"). It links a person to the thing or person they own, know, or are related to.

🎂 Asking and saying someone's age

Nan sai desu ka? ("How old are you?") is the standard way to ask age — sai is the counter word that attaches to a number to mean "years old," similar to how English says "25 years old" rather than just "25." Nijuu go sai desu — "I am 25 years old."

🏠 Saying where you live

[Place] ni sunde imasu ("I live in [place]") uses the particle ni to mark the place where someone lives, and sunde imasu ("live/am living") as the verb phrase. Doko ni sunde imasu ka? asks "Where do you live?"

🐾 Saying you have someone or something (imasu)

Imasu is used to say you "have" or there "is" a person or animal: Ani ga imasu ("I have an older brother"), Neko ga imasu ("I have a cat"). This is different from objects, which use a separate verb (arimasu) covered in a later unit.

Imouto ga imasu. — I have a younger sister.

The particle to means "and" when connecting nouns: Chichi to haha — my father and mother.

2. Saying someone's relationship to you

Watashi no haha desu. — She is my mother.

Watashi no tomodachi desu. — He or she is my friend.

Watashi no petto no Momo desu. — This is my pet, Momo.

The particle no shows possession or connection: Watashi no haha — my mother; Yuki-san no tomodachi — Yuki's friend.

3. Asking and saying someone's age

Nan sai desu ka? — How old are you?

Nijuu go sai desu. — I am 25 years old.

Kodomo wa nan sai desu ka? — How old is your child?

Go sai desu. — He or she is five years old.

The counter sai is used for age.

4. Saying where you live

Tokyo ni sunde imasu. — I live in Tokyo.

Berlin ni sunde imasu. — I live in Berlin.

Kazoku wa Ankara ni sunde imasu. — My family lives in Ankara.

The particle ni marks the place where someone lives.

5. Asking where someone lives

Doko ni sunde imasu ka? — Where do you live?

Kazoku wa doko ni sunde imasu ka? — Where does your family live?

Istanbul ni sunde imasu. — I live in Istanbul.

6. The particles "to" and "no"

To connects nouns ("and"): Chichi to haha — my father and mother; Tomodachi to kodomo — a friend and a child.

No shows possession or relationship: Watashi no ane — my older sister; Yamada-san no inu — Mr. Yamada's dog.

7. "Sunde imasu" and "imasu"

Sunde imasu means "live" or "am living": Osaka ni sunde imasu — I live in Osaka; Nihon ni sunde imasu — I live in Japan.

Imasu is used for people and animals: Ani ga imasu — I have an older brother; Neko ga imasu — I have a cat. For objects, Japanese uses arimasu instead, studied in a later unit.

Japanese has two sets of family words — plain terms for talking about your own family (chichi, haha, ani, ane) and more polite terms for someone else's family, or for addressing your own parents or older siblings directly (otousan, okaasan, onii-san, onee-san). This unit covers naming foods and drinks, saying what you like and dislike, asking about someone's preferences, and talking about what you usually eat, drink, and how often.

😋 Saying what you like

[Food] ga suki desu ("I like [food]") uses the particle ga before suki ("like") or daisuki ("like very much") — notice that suki behaves like an adjective here, not a verb, so it pairs with ga rather than the object particle o.

🙅 Saying what you dislike

[Food] ga kirai desu states a dislike directly, but kirai can sound quite blunt. [Food] wa suki ja nai desu ("I don't like [food]") is a softer, more common way to say the same thing — swapping ga for wa and negating suki instead of using the stronger word kirai.

❓ Asking about food preferences

[Food] ga suki desu ka? asks "Do you like [food]?" Nani ga suki desu ka? ("What do you like?") and Donna tabemono ga suki desu ka? ("What kind of food do you like?") are more open versions of the same question.

🍴 Saying what you eat and drink

Unlike suki, eating and drinking are real action verbs, so they take the object particle o: [Food] o tabemasu ("I eat [food]") and [Drink] o nomimasu ("I drink [drink]"). O marks the thing being eaten or drunk, just as it will mark the object of most action verbs you learn later.

🔁 Talking about frequency

Yoku ("often"), tokidoki ("sometimes"), and amari ("not very much") go before the verb to say how often you do something. Amari is special: it's almost always paired with a negative verb ending (-masen), as in Amari koohii o nomimasen — "I don't drink coffee very often."

Directness about dislikes is softened in Japanese the same way politeness is softened elsewhere: saying kirai ("I hate it") about food can sound unexpectedly strong, especially to a host who cooked for you, so suki ja nai desu ("it's not really my thing") is the more commonly used, gentler phrasing in everyday conversation — the same instinct that makes English speakers say "I'm not really a fan of..." instead of "I hate...".

This unit covers reading basic menu items, ordering food and drinks politely, saying what you choose, ordering a quantity, and asking whether something is available.

🙏 Ordering something politely

[Item] o kudasai and [Item] onegaishimasu both mean "[Item], please," and either is fine for ordering — kudasai ("please give me") attaches after the object particle o, while onegaishimasu can follow the item directly without o. Both are equally polite; which one you hear more depends on the speaker and the shop.

🍛 Saying what you choose

[Item] ni shimasu ("I'll have [item]") is used specifically when choosing from a menu or a set of options — it literally means "I'll decide on [item]," which is a more natural way to announce a choice than simply naming the food with desu.

🔢 Ordering a quantity

The basic counters hitotsu, futatsu, mittsu ("one, two, three items") go before kudasai or onegaishimasu to specify how many you want: Edamame futatsu kudasai — "Two portions of edamame, please." These generic counters work for most items, though some foods and drinks use their own specific counters you'll meet later.

❓ Asking whether something is available

[Item] arimasu ka? ("Do you have [item]? / Is there [item]?") is how you check a menu item exists before ordering it — useful when something isn't listed clearly or you're not sure. The particle mo ("too/also") is handy for adding to an order: Mizu mo onegaishimasu — "Water too, please."

Irasshaimase ("welcome!") is called out by staff the moment you walk into almost any restaurant or shop in Japan — you're not expected to reply to it. To get a server's attention at the table, a clear "sumimasen" works well; many restaurants also have a call button at the table instead. Kashikomarimashita is a very polite way staff acknowledge your order (literally "I have humbly received it"), and tipping is not customary in Japan — the price on the menu is the price you pay.

This unit covers naming rooms and objects in a home, saying what a room is, saying what's there (or not), and describing a home with simple adjectives.

🚪 Saying what a place is

Koko wa [place] desu ("This is [place]") identifies where you are, the same desu pattern used for names and jobs in earlier units — just with koko ("here") as the topic instead of watashi.

📦 Saying something is in a place

[Place] ni [thing] ga arimasu ("There is [thing] in [place]") states that an object exists somewhere — arimasu is the "have/there is" verb for objects (as opposed to imasu, used for people and animals, from Unit 4). Ni marks the location, and ga marks the thing that's there.

🚫 Saying something is not there

[Thing] wa arimasen is the negative of arimasu — "there is no [thing]." The topic particle wa is typically used instead of ga once you're saying what's absent.

🏡 Describing a home or room

Simple descriptive words like hiroi ("spacious"), semai ("small/narrow"), shizuka ("quiet"), atarashii ("new") and furui ("old") attach to desu the same way: [Place] wa [adjective] desu.

🙅 Making adjectives negative

Japanese adjectives split into two types with different negative forms. I-adjectives (ending in -i, like hiroi, furui) drop the final i and add -ku nai desu: hiroi → hiroku nai desu. Na-adjectives (like shizuka) behave like nouns and just add ja nai desu, the same negative pattern you've already seen with nationality and job: shizuka → shizuka ja nai desu.
